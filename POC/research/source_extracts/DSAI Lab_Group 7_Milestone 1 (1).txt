1. Introduction and Problem Definition
Satellites photograph the same places on Earth again and again. Comparing two images of one place,
taken months or years apart, shows how it has changed: a new building, a cleared plot, a widened road.
Indian cities and government bodies already use this to catch unauthorised construction and
encroachment on public land [3][4].
Most automated tools stop at a change map, a black-and-white mask that marks which pixels changed.
A mask tells an officer where to look, but not what changed or whether it matters. Someone still has to
open every flagged area and interpret it. Many flags are not real changes at all: shadows move, the sun
angle shifts, vegetation changes with the season, and the two images may be slightly misaligned or
captured by different sensors.
Recent vision-language models can describe a change in a full sentence, which looks like the missing
piece. However, they are known to state things the images do not support, such as the wrong number
of buildings, objects that are not there, or causes that nobody could know from two pictures [7][17]. For
a user who has to act on the description, a fluent but wrong sentence is worse than no sentence.
Rather than building yet another detector, this project focuses on turning detected change into a
description that can be trusted: one that is localised, says what changed, and only claims what the
imagery supports.
Problem Statement
How can two satellite images of the same place be turned into a localised, plain-language
account of what changed, one that only says what the imagery supports, and that holds up when
the images differ for reasons that are not real change, such as lighting, season or alignment?
We focus on urban and built-up change: new or removed buildings, new roads and groundwork. This
is where good labelled data exists, where there are real users today, and where every claim the system
makes can be checked against a label.

There is far more imagery than people to check it. The Sentinel-2 mission alone publishes more
than 4 TB of new products every day [1], and commercial and national satellites add much more. No

Users already pay for flag-and-verify systems. In July 2026, Mumbai's municipal corporation
approved a system that combines AI, drones and satellite imagery to flag illegal construction and extra
floors early [3]. Since 2022, India's Directorate General Defence Estates has run change-detection
software in all 62 cantonment boards, comparing Cartosat-3 images from different dates [4]. In both
cases the software raises a flag and an officer then checks it on the ground.
The expensive step is the human check. Every false flag costs an officer time. Every flag without
context ("something changed here") must be interpreted before anyone can act. A tool that marks real
change and says plainly what it is, without overstating, directly reduces that cost.
Common scenarios where this problem arises include:
a ward officer reviewing new construction in their area every quarter
an estate officer checking for encroachment on government land
a planner comparing growth across neighbourhoods
a researcher deciding whether a change-captioning model can be trusted
5. Objectives
The project has four objectives. Each has a metric and a comparison fixed in advance. The targets are
hypotheses we will test, not promised results.
1. O1: Detect where buildings and roads changed. Train standard change detectors and reproduce
published results on public benchmarks, measured by change-class F1 and IoU.
2. O2: Ignore changes that are not real. Make the detector robust to nuisance differences
(brightness, colour cast, blur, compression, small misalignment), measured by how much F1 drops
on perturbed test images.
3. O3: Describe the change without overstating. Generate descriptions that use the change map as
evidence and are checked against it, measured by human-judged factual accuracy and count error
as well as standard text metrics.
4. O4: Make it usable. Deliver a working demo with measured end-to-end latency.
The quantitative targets for each objective are given in Section 9.4.
6. Existing Approaches
Research on change detection in remote sensing has moved from simple image differencing, through
supervised deep networks, to foundation models and vision-language systems that also describe the
change. The approaches fall into six broad families, followed here by the operational tools currently in
use.

6.1. Classical Change Detection
The oldest approach subtracts one image from the other, or compares spectral vectors pixel by pixel
(change vector analysis), and applies a threshold such as Otsu's method. Morphological filtering then
removes small noise. These methods are fast, need no training data, and are easy to explain.
Limitation: Any difference between the two images counts as change. Moving shadows,
seasonal colour and small misalignment all appear as false positives. The output also says
nothing about what changed.
6.2. Siamese Convolutional Networks
Deep learning replaced hand-set thresholds with learned features. Daudt et al. introduced fully
convolutional early-fusion and Siamese networks (FC-EF, FC-Siam-conc and FC-Siam-diff), which
encode both images and compare their features to predict a change mask [8]. U-Net-style encoder-
decoders with a feature-difference step remain strong and widely used baselines.
Limitation: These models need pixel-level labels and learn only the kinds of change their
training set labels. Their output is still only a mask.
6.3. Transformer-Based Models
Transformers model long-range relationships between the two dates. BIT represents each image with a
small set of semantic tokens and lets a transformer compare them [9]. ChangeFormer uses a
hierarchical transformer encoder in a Siamese setup and reports 90.40 F1 on LEVIR-CD [5].
Limitation: Gains over CNNs are small. A 2024 study that retrained recent methods under a
fixed protocol found that a plain U-Net "without training tricks or complicated architectural
changes is still a top performer" [6]. Detection accuracy on the main benchmarks is close to
saturated.
6.4. Foundation and Zero-Shot Models
Newer work avoids task-specific training. AnyChange adapts the Segment Anything Model to find
changes with no training and reports a new best result for unsupervised change detection on SECOND
[10]. RemoteCLIP aligns remote-sensing images with text, which allows zero-shot labelling and retrieval
[11].
Limitation: Zero-shot detection still trails supervised models on standard benchmarks, and these
models also produce masks or labels, not explanations.
6.5. Change Captioning
Change captioning generates a sentence describing the difference between two images. The LEVIR-
CC benchmark provides 10,077 image pairs with 50,385 human-written sentences [12]. RSICCformer
[12] and Chg2Cap [13] are the standard trained baselines.

Limitation: Captioners are trained and scored with n-gram overlap metrics such as BLEU and
CIDEr, which reward sounding like the reference rather than being correct. A caption can score
well and still get the count or location wrong. The captioner also does not use the change map,
even when one is available.
6.6. Integrated Systems and Vision-Language Models
Change-Agent is the closest precedent to this project. Its MCI model trains a change-detection branch
and a captioning branch together on LEVIR-MCI and places a large language model on top for
interactive analysis [7]. ChangeChat instruction-tunes a vision-language model to answer questions
about image pairs [14]. CCExpert adds a difference-aware module to a multimodal LLM and pretrains it
on 200,000 image pairs [15].
Limitation: These systems are still judged mainly on n-gram scores, and large models can
produce fluent but unsupported statements. Their training data increasingly includes LLM-
generated captions, which can carry errors of their own [15]. Ricci et al. (2026) evaluated general
vision-language models for change captioning and proposed FMScore, an LLM-based check of
captions against ground-truth facts [17]. The field has recognised the factuality problem, but it
has not been solved.

6.8. Baseline System
The baseline considered in this project reflects the common state of practice. It consists of:
a trained change detector (Siamese U-Net or ChangeFormer) that produces a change mask, and
a trained change captioner (Chg2Cap or RSICCformer) that sees only the two images.
The two components run independently, and nothing checks whether the sentence agrees with the
mask. This baseline is the reference point for testing whether giving the captioner the change map, and

6.9. Evaluation Practices in Prior Research
Prior work evaluates each task separately, on fixed benchmark splits.
Change detection is measured with precision, recall, F1 and intersection-over-union (IoU) of the
change class, sometimes with overall accuracy. Overall accuracy is not very informative here: most
pixels do not change, so a model that predicts "no change" everywhere still scores high.
Multi-class change masks, such as LEVIR-MCI's background, road and building classes, are
measured with mean IoU (mIoU).
Semantic change detection on SECOND uses mIoU and the separated kappa coefficient (SeK).
Change captioning is measured with BLEU-1 to BLEU-4, METEOR, ROUGE-L and CIDEr-D
against reference sentences.
In all of these, success means a higher score on the benchmark's test split. Whether a generated
description is factually correct is rarely measured; FMScore [17] is one of the first attempts. Our
evaluation therefore keeps the standard metrics for comparability, and adds factual accuracy as the
primary measure for descriptions.
7. Gaps and Proposed Contribution
The review shows a common pattern across all families of approaches. Detection keeps improving, but
the step that matters to the user, a description that can be trusted, is still weak. Five specific gaps stand
out:
1. Detection is close to saturated. The top methods are within about 4 F1 points of each other on
LEVIR-CD, and a well-trained U-Net is still near the top [5][6]. Another detector is unlikely to be a
meaningful contribution.
2. Descriptions are not checked for truth. Captioners are scored on n-gram overlap, so a sentence
with the wrong number of buildings can still score well. Factual evaluation is only now being
proposed [17].
3. Captioners ignore available evidence. Most captioning models never see the change map.
Change-Agent trains both tasks together, but its captions are evaluated with n-gram metrics and
nothing checks the sentence against the predicted mask [7].
4. Robustness to nuisance change is rarely reported. Methods perform much worse on
S2Looking's side-looking images than on near-nadir LEVIR-CD+ [19], yet most papers report only
clean, in-domain scores.
5. Large models are hard to run and hard to trust. Vision-language systems need large GPUs and
are increasingly trained on generated captions [15], which makes errors harder to trace.
This highlights a clear opportunity: a system that keeps a strong, standard detector, uses its change
map as explicit evidence for the description, checks the description against that evidence, and reports
how often the description is actually correct, as judged by people, alongside the usual scores.

Nature of the contribution. Our contribution lies in reliability (factual correctness) and robustness,
not in raw detection accuracy. Compared with Change-Agent [7], we keep detection and captioning as
separate, replaceable modules; pass the predicted mask to the captioner as structured evidence; add a
claim-level consistency check; and evaluate descriptions with human-judged factual accuracy and count
error. We do not claim that combining detection and captioning is new.
8. Proposed Approach and Technical Grounding
8.1. System Components
The proposed system converts a pair of images into a change map and a description grounded in that
map. At a high level it has five components.
Input and quality checks. The two images are loaded as a pair, checked for size and alignment,
and normalised. On benchmark data the images are already co-registered; the checks catch broken
or mismatched pairs.
Change detector. A standard supervised model, a Siamese U-Net and ChangeFormer [5], predicts
a pixel-level change probability map, which is thresholded into a mask. The threshold is chosen on
validation data only.
Class Imbalance & Loss Strategy: In satellite change detection benchmarks, unchanged background
pixels comprise 95%–99% of image tiles. Standard Cross-Entropy leads to degenerate models that
predict "no change" everywhere. To overcome this, our training pipeline implements:
1. Composite Loss Function: A weighted combination of Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE), Focal Loss (to
suppress gradients from easily classified background pixels), and Soft Dice / Focal Tversky Loss (to
directly maximize change-class region overlap):
2. Change-Centric Patch Sampling: Batches are sampled with a 70:30 ratio between tiles containing
verified change events and negative background tiles, preventing gradient starvation.
3. Invariance Augmentations: To meet Objective O2, the detector is trained with bitemporal jitter
(independent brightness and contrast shifts, colour casts, Gaussian blur, JPEG compression, and
small spatial affine misalignments of ±2 pixels between dates), forcing the network to disregard
illumination and sensor artifacts.
4. Change events. The predicted mask is converted into a list of change events. Each event is a
connected region with its class (building or road on LEVIR-MCI), size in pixels, approximate position
in the image (for example "lower left") and the total count per class. This list is the evidence the rest
of the pipeline relies on.
5. Grounded captioner. A captioning model generates the description from the two images and the
change events. We start from established captioning architectures (Chg2Cap, RSICCformer) [12]
[13] and add the events as an extra input. A simple template-based description from the events
alone serves as a lower baseline.

8.3. Data Quality and Known Risks
Several issues will be audited in Milestone 2:
Overlap within the LEVIR family. LEVIR-MCI uses the LEVIR-CC images, and ChangeChat-105k
reuses them too. Training on one set and testing on another could leak test images. Each
experiment will use one dataset's official split, and images will be hashed to detect duplicates across
sets.
Partial labels. LEVIR-CD labels only buildings, and LEVIR-MCI adds roads. A real but unlabelled
change looks like a false positive, so results are reported per dataset and unlabelled changes are
counted separately.
SECOND test labels. Only 2,968 of the 4,662 pairs are openly available [21], so SECOND would
need a scene-level re-split, or it will be deferred.
Different sensors and viewing angles. S2Looking is side-looking and rural, and SYSU-CD is
aerial imagery of Hong Kong. Their scores are not comparable with LEVIR's, so they are used only
as separate robustness and transfer tests.
Licence terms. LEVIR-CD images come from Google Earth and may be used for academic
purposes only [16]. The shared copy stays private and all sources are cited.
Resolution gap. Models trained at 0.5 m do not transfer to 10–30 m Sentinel-2 or HLS imagery [2].
Any example on such imagery will be a clearly labelled demonstration, not a result.
8.4. Hardware and Compute Assumptions
Change detectors at 256×256 resolution train comfortably on a single consumer GPU or a free Colab or
Kaggle T4 GPU, using mixed precision. The caption baselines have public code and similar
requirements. Large vision-language models are optional and would be used only for inference. Latency
is reported on stated hardware for the full pipeline.

9. Testing and Evaluation Strategy
9.1. Experimental Setup
All models are trained on the official training split of each dataset, tuned on validation data, and
evaluated once on the held-out test split. The experiments answer five questions:
Can we detect change as well as published work? Absolute differencing with an Otsu threshold,
a Siamese U-Net and ChangeFormer are compared on LEVIR-CD (F1, IoU) and LEVIR-MCI
(mIoU).
Does robustness training help? The plain and robustness-trained detectors are compared on
clean and perturbed test images: five perturbation types (brightness and contrast, colour cast, blur,
JPEG compression, small misalignment) at three severities each.
Does it generalise? Models trained on LEVIR are tested on S2Looking and SYSU-CD.
Does the change map make descriptions more truthful? Four caption settings are compared on
the same test pairs: (i) images only, (ii) images plus the predicted change map, (iii) images plus the
ground-truth map, reported as an upper bound only, and (iv) setting (ii) plus the consistency check.
Is it practical? End-to-end latency and GPU memory are measured for the full pipeline.
9.2. Evaluation Metrics
Change-class F1 and IoU (primary for detection), with precision and recall; mIoU on LEVIR-MCI.
F1 drop under perturbation, averaged across perturbation types and severities (robustness).
Supported-claim precision: the share of claims in a description that a human judges supported by
the images and mask (primary for descriptions).
Hallucination rate (unsupported or contradicted claims per description) and omission rate
(labelled changes not mentioned).
Count Absolute Error (CAE): The mean absolute difference between the count of structures stated

9.3. Human Factual Audit
Descriptions are audited by people, because n-gram metrics cannot judge correctness.
A random sample of at least 100 LEVIR-MCI test pairs, the same pairs for every system.
Two annotators per description, blind to which system wrote it. Each claim is marked supported,
unsupported or contradicted by the images and mask.
Agreement between annotators is reported with Cohen's kappa, and a third team member resolves
disagreements.

To keep the results honest, splits and primary metrics are fixed before final testing, thresholds and
prompts are chosen on validation data only, the main models are run with three random seeds where
compute allows, and confidence intervals are computed by bootstrapping over test pairs. The ground-
truth-mask setting is reported as an upper bound, never as an achievable result. Negative results will be
reported.
9.5. Evaluation Objective
The objective of this evaluation is to measure whether giving a captioning model the change map as
evidence, and checking its output against that evidence, makes change descriptions more truthful
without making them less fluent. By comparing the grounded system against an image-only baseline on
identical test pairs and judging factual accuracy by people, the evaluation isolates the effect of evidence
grounding on the quality that matters to the user.
